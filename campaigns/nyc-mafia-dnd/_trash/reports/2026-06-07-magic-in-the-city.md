# A-2 — Magic Visibility Rules

**Date:** 2026-06-07
**Campaign:** NYC Mafia × D&D
**Item:** A-2 — Magic visibility rules (public / hidden / licensed)

---

## Recommendation: **Licensed-but-hidden** (layered model)

Magic is not public-knowledge. It is not fully hidden either. It sits in a **bureaucratic grey zone** where the official story is "stagecraft, fraud, and urban legend" while actual supernatural activity is **permitted, tracked, and taxed** through a licensing framework that most citizens don't know exists.

This is the single most load-bearing worldbuilding decision for the campaign. Everything from faction power to session pacing flows from it.

---

## The three public layers

| Layer | Who knows | What they see |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| **Street** | Civilians | Lucky charms, card tricks, that "weird guy" downtown. Confirmation bias does the heavy lifting. A fireball in an alley is a gas leak by morning. |
| **Grey market** | Fixers, cops, families | Magic is real, magic is regulated, magic is a commodity. Enchanted burner phones are just "good hardware." Divination is "market research." |
| **Deep** | Commission, OCU, the Below | Planar bleed, pact magic, necromantic waste. Not licensed — *managed*. Things the ALB has no form for. |

### Why this model works for the table

1. **Factions have leverage.** If magic were public, the Commission and ALB would be irrelevant bureaucracies. If it were fully hidden, there'd be no licensing board, no legal permits, no leverage for the Ashford family's warlock consultants. The grey zone is where organized crime thrives — controlling access to something that *exists but isn't acknowledged*.

2. **Content escalation is built in.** Early sessions can play magic as rumor and suspicion (did that guy actually roll a 30, or is something else going on?). By mid-campaign the table knows the rules and can navigate the licensing system. Deep-layer encounters are horror-tinged by contrast.

3. **NYC cop realism survives.** The OCU exists but is underfunded (per `factions.md`). They can't investigate every cold reading in the Village. Magic crime gets attention only when it crosses RICO thresholds or makes the news. This keeps the police pressure *realistic without being overwhelming*.

---

## The licensing framework (brief)

The **Arcane Licensing Board (ALB)** issues permits for:
- **Healing services** (heavily taxed; Moretti family controls underground clinics)
- **Divination consultation** (Chen-Okafor "market research" front)
- **Warding and enchantment** (Ashford corporate "security consulting")
- **Experimental/unclassified** (the gap the Kowalski necromancy taboo falls into)

**Unlicensed magic** is a felony — but enforcement is sparse, corrupted, and selective. The mob doesn't prevent enforcement; they *steer* it. Getting a rival's alchemist arrested for an expired permit is a Tuesday afternoon.

**The practical table rule:** NPCs react to magic the way NYC reacts to anything technically-illegal-but-everyone-does-it. Cops look the other way for the right price. Civilians rationalize. Only when something bleeds (literally or in the press) does the system kick in.

---

## What PCs see at session 1

The crew doesn't need to know all this on day one. They should understand:
- Magic is *around* — maybe they've used a magic-adjacent service without knowing
- The families treat it as infrastructure (utilities, not miracles)
- Getting caught doing the wrong magic in the wrong borough is the major risk

The full licensing mechanics come out through play, through faction interaction, and through the OCU scene in whatever the opening job becomes.
